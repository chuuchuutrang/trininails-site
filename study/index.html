Study smarter.

A personal guide from Bitchley on how to study like a lazy person—less busywork, better memory, and proof you actually learned it.

The main rule

Do not measure studying by how many hours you spend. Measure it by what you can explain, recall, or solve without looking at your materials.

Simple routine

After class: Expand notes, write 3–5 questions, mark one confusing point, and update your cheat sheet.

Evening: Do one focused block, retrieve, correct, and briefly review older material.

Weekly: Take a cumulative quiz, mix chapters, update your error log, and schedule reviews.

  5. 25–30 Summarize and schedule the next review.

01Set directionStart with what the class requires

Check the syllabus, slides, notes, learning objectives, study guides, homework, previous quizzes, and topics your instructor repeats. Not every textbook sentence deserves equal attention.

Choose a concrete target

  • Explain cellular respiration without notes.
  • Solve five derivative problems.
  • Memorize twenty vocabulary terms.
  • Outline a possible essay response.

Replace “study Chapter 5” with something you can prove you completed.

02Save timeLook for existing cheat sheets first

Search course sites, shared folders, Quizlet, student platforms, department pages, public notes, publisher resources, and tutoring centers. Existing guides reveal major topics, simpler explanations, examples, and gaps.

My personal favorite is Google Images. Search the subject plus “cheat sheet,” “study guide,” or “one-page summary.” Image results make it fast to scan many layouts, comparison charts, diagrams, and visual summaries before choosing the most useful sources. Start with the exact class name or course number. If that is unfruitful, search the textbook title, chapter name, or broader course name instead—for example, switch from “BIO 121 Professor Smith” to “Campbell Biology Chapter 10” or “introductory biology cellular respiration cheat sheet.”

Verify everything against your professor’s slides, syllabus, assigned readings, homework, and your own notes. Rewrite useful information in your own words.

Use a cheat sheet in an exam only when it is explicitly allowed. Never use leaked exams or submit someone else’s work.

03Read strategicallyHow to read a textbook—or not

Your goal is to locate, understand, and remember what matters—not automatically read every page.

Use the textbook when

  • Lecture is unclear or you need another example.
  • A section is assigned or homework cites it.
  • You need a definition, formula, or detailed process.
  • It contains material not covered in class.

Use it as a reference when

  • Slides already explain the topic clearly.
  • The chapter repeats material you understand.
  • You only need to clarify specific points.
  • A guide identifies the exact concepts needed.
04Preview firstScan before you read

Preview objectives, headings, bold terms, definitions, diagrams, charts, captions, formulas, worked examples, summaries, and review questions.

For each bold term, ask

  • What does it mean and why is it important?
  • How does it connect to the chapter?
  • Could I explain it without reading the definition?
05Give reading a purposeTurn headings into questions

Turn “The Stages of Mitosis” into “What are the stages, and what happens in each?” Turn “Causes of Inflation” into “What causes inflation, and how do the causes differ?”

Read only far enough to answer. Then close the book and answer in your own words.

06Protect attentionSkip the noise

Move quickly through

  • Repeated explanations and long introductions.
  • Background, sidebars, and examples you understand.
  • Material unrelated to learning objectives.

Slow down for

  • Definitions, formulas, diagrams, and comparisons.
  • Multi-step processes and worked problems.
  • Emphasized ideas you cannot explain yourself.

Skipping noise means spending attention where it has the highest value—not avoiding difficult material.

07Find the signalUse search on digital material

Use Ctrl F on Windows or ⌘ F on Mac. Search lecture terms, study-guide words, assignment keywords, names, theories, formulas, dates, objectives, and homework questions.

Try alternate wording, and read around each match for context.

08A complete study loopUse short, focused study blocks
  1. 25–30 min

    Focus

    Put your phone away, close unrelated tabs, prepare materials, and choose one clear target.

  2. 5 min

    Retrieve

    Close everything. Write, explain, draw, solve, outline, or code from memory.

  3. Check

    Correct

    Label your work correct, incomplete, incorrect, or blank. Study the gaps.

  4. 5 min

    Reset

    Stand, stretch, walk, drink water, and rest your eyes. Take a longer break after two or three rounds.

09Make memory workActive recall

Retrieve before looking at the answer. Use practice tests, flashcards, brain dumps, blank-page summaries, problems, teach-backs, diagrams, and outlines.

Strong flashcards: give the full answer and explain it before flipping. The struggle to retrieve is part of learning.

10Remember longerSpaced practice
Day 0LearnDay 1RetrieveDay 3Review gapsDay 7RetrieveDay 14Test again

Review weak material more often and strong material less often. Start before the night prior to the exam.

11Choose the methodInterleaving

Mix related topics or problem types instead of doing one large block of identical questions. Try addition → multiplication → subtraction → division, rather than finishing every addition problem first.

It feels harder because you must decide which method applies—and that makes the practice more useful.

12Expose the gapsTeach-back method

Explain the material to a total beginner. Answer: What is it? How does it work? Why does it matter? What is an example? What is it confused with?

Where your explanation gets fuzzy, return to the source only long enough to fill the gap. Then explain it again without looking.

13A reading frameworkSQ3R for textbook chapters
  1. S

    Survey

    Preview headings, objectives, terms, diagrams, summaries, and questions.

  2. Q

    Question

    Turn headings into questions.

  3. R

    Read

    Read to find the answers.

  4. R

    Recite

    Close the book and answer from memory.

  5. R

    Review

    Return later with spaced practice.

The most important step is recitation. Reading without retrieval is usually not enough.

14Build connectionsElaboration and examples

Ask why it happens, how A differs from B, what changes under a new condition, when you would use it, how it connects to earlier material, and what mistake someone might make.

Give abstract ideas concrete examples. Add diagrams to verbal explanations, then reproduce both from memory.

15Distill the courseCreate a one-page cheat sheet

Include essential terms, short definitions, formulas and when to use them, processes, comparisons, mistakes, hard examples, diagrams, memory tricks, and unanswered questions.

Use limited space deliberately: short phrases, arrows, personal abbreviations, diagrams, comparison charts, and numbered processes. Give the most space to what is difficult or easily confused.

16Keep notes aliveBetter note-taking workflow

During class

Capture main ideas, definitions, examples, questions, emphasis, and connections—not every sentence.

Right after

Fill context, clarify wording, mark questions, make connections, and create practice prompts.

Before next class

Close your notes, write what you remember, then check accuracy.

18Learn from missesKeep an error log

Record the question or topic, your answer, the correct answer, why you missed it, and how you will avoid that mistake next time.

Watch for misreading, forgotten formulas, confused terms, skipped steps, wrong methods, and difficulty applying something you understand. Review the log before the exam.

19Common trapsWhat to avoid
  • Passive rereadingClose, recall, check, correct.
  • Excessive highlightingMark only key ideas, then turn them into questions.
  • Copying solved examplesCover the solution and reproduce it.
  • CrammingUse several short sessions over multiple days.
  • Studying distractedRemove the distraction instead of relying on willpower.
  • Sacrificing sleepRest often beats one more exhausted hour.
  • Collecting resourcesChoose one, verify it, improve it, and test yourself.
20The final check“Do I really know it?”

You know it when you can explain it without notes, create an original example, compare it with a related idea, understand unfamiliar wording, apply it to a new problem, recall it days later, and correct a common mistake.

Recognizing the answer when you see it is not enough.

21When exams feel too shortPractice reading with speed and control

Reading speed is trainable. I used to read around 100 words per minute. An after-school program helped me reach up to 1,000 WPM, and about 500 WPM with 90% comprehension. The useful goal is not maximum speed—it is the fastest pace at which you still understand what the question is asking.

Build speed without losing meaning

  1. 1

    Find your baseline

    Read a timed passage, calculate your WPM, then answer comprehension questions without looking back.

  2. 2

    Guide your eyes

    Use a finger, pen, or cursor to maintain a steady pace and reduce unnecessary rereading.

  3. 3

    Read in phrases

    Practice taking in short groups of words rather than silently focusing on every word by itself.

  4. 4

    Vary your speed

    Move quickly through examples and familiar context; slow down for definitions, constraints, exceptions, and unfamiliar reasoning.

  5. 5

    Retest comprehension

    Increase speed gradually, but lower it whenever recall or accuracy drops below your target.

Practice for timed exams

  • Preview the entire exam and note point values before starting.
  • Translate the time limit into a rough time budget per section.
  • Read the question first, then identify command words such as compare, calculate, or explain.
  • Mark difficult questions and return after collecting easier points.
  • Practice with the same format and time pressure before exam day.

Track two numbers: words per minute and comprehension percentage. Speed only counts when understanding stays high.

When progress feels slow

You can become far more capable than you are today.

Encouragement works best when it gives you something honest to do next. Start small, practice with purpose, and improve the conditions around your effort.

01 · Kaizen

Start with one minute.

The Japanese idea of kaizen is commonly translated as continuous improvement. Make the first change small enough to repeat. One honest minute today lowers the barrier to returning tomorrow. The win is not intensity; the win is becoming someone who starts.

02 · Talent

A head start is not a destiny.

You do not need to feel talented before you begin. Natural ease may shape the first few tries; practice, feedback, curiosity, and persistence shape what comes next. Talent is not a ceiling.

03 · 500 hours

Focused time changes you.

Five hundred focused hours is not a magic guarantee of mastery, but it can make you remarkably capable in a defined skill. That is about one hour a day for sixteen months—or thirty minutes a day for nearly three years.

04 · Outliers

Success is never talent alone.

Outliers argues that achievement is shaped by practice and also by timing, culture, opportunity, access, support, and luck. Do the work—and seek environments that give your work room to compound.

05 · Access

Build the conditions.

Ask for feedback. Find a mentor or study partner. Use libraries, office hours, free courses, borrowed tools, and communities. Barriers are real; they are not personal failure. Better access helps effort become progress.

06 · Possibility

Your future is larger than today’s evidence.

You cannot guarantee every outcome, but you can create more options. Begin, learn, ask, adjust, and begin again. Your present skill shows where you are—not where you must stop.

Make the hours count: focus, challenge, feedback, correction, rest, and consistency determine what practice produces.
